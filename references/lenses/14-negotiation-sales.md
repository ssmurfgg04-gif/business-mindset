# Lens 14: Negotiation & Sales — How Do I Close Deals and Win Terms?

<lens>
<core_question>
How do I sell this opportunity to buyers, investors, partners, and talent —
and how do I negotiate terms that capture maximum value without killing the deal?
</core_question>

<when_to_use>
- User asks "how do I sell this?" or "how do I close deals?"
- User is preparing for a sales conversation, pitch, or negotiation
- User is negotiating a term sheet, partnership, or employment offer
- User wants to improve cold outreach reply rates
- User is designing a sales process or hiring salespeople
- After Lens 09 (Pricing) confirms pricing, before Lens 13 (Growth) scales it
</when_to_use>

<when_not_to_use>
- Pre-product (nothing to sell yet)
- Pure product/market analysis (no deal on the table)
- Internal team dynamics (use Lens 12 + operator-personas instead)
</when_not_to_use>
</lens>

## Core Philosophy

<principle>
Most founders treat sales as a necessary evil. The best operators treat it as
the highest-leverage skill in business. Every conversation is a negotiation —
with customers, investors, employees, partners, suppliers. The operator who
negotiates 10% better on every deal compounds 10% advantages into orders of
magnitude over a career.

Two principles govern this lens:
1. **Tactical empathy** (Voss): Understanding the other side's constraints is
   not weakness — it's intelligence. You can't influence someone you don't
   understand.
2. **Principled negotiation** (Ury): Focus on interests, not positions. The
   best deals expand the pie, not just divide it.
</principle>

## Part 1: Negotiation Frameworks

<negotiation_frameworks>

### Framework A: Voss — Tactical Empathy (FBI hostage negotiation applied to business)

Chris Voss's "Never Split the Difference" — synthesized from 24 years as FBI
lead international kidnapping negotiator. The techniques work because hostage
negotiation is the extreme case: zero trust, zero leverage, life-or-death stakes.

#### 1. Mirroring
Repeat the last 1-3 words of what the other side said, as a question. Forces
them to elaborate, reveals information, builds rapport.

**Example**:
- Them: "We can't justify that price point in this budget cycle."
- You: "In this budget cycle?"
- Them: "Yeah, our fiscal year resets in March, so any new spend has to wait until Q2."

#### 2. Labeling
Name the other side's emotion to defuse it. "It seems like..." or "It sounds
like..." — not "I understand how you feel" (presumptuous).

**Example**: "It seems like you're under pressure from leadership to reduce
vendor spend this quarter." Forces them to confirm or correct — either way,
you learn their constraint.

#### 3. Calibrated Questions
Open-ended questions starting with "what" or "how" that force the other side
to solve your problem. Never "why" (sounds accusatory).

**Examples**:
- "How am I supposed to do that?" (forces them to consider your constraints)
- "What is the biggest challenge you face with your current solution?"
- "How would you like me to proceed?"
- "What's the objective behind this requirement?" (reveals interest behind position)

#### 4. The Accusation Audit
Before the negotiation, list every bad thing the other side could think about
you. Say them first. Defuses them.

**Example**: "Before we start, you probably think I'm another salesperson who's
going to waste your time, overpromise, and disappear after you sign. I get it.
Most software vendors do exactly that."

#### 5. "No" is the new "Yes"
Push for "no" early. "No" makes people feel safe and in control. "Is now a
bad time to talk?" gets more calls started than "Do you have a few minutes?"

**The 3 types of "yes"**:
- Counterfeit yes (they want to escape the conversation)
- Confirming yes (fact-checking, no commitment)
- Commitment yes (real agreement)

Only the commitment yes counts. Push for "no" to surface objections.

#### 6. "That's right"
The magic words. When the other side says "that's right," they've conceded
you understand their position. Real agreement follows. Get them to "that's
right" by summarizing their position better than they could.

#### 7. The Black Swan rule
Every negotiation has 3+ unknown unknowns (black swans) that, if discovered,
change the deal. Your job is to surface them. Listen for inconsistencies,
offhand comments, emotional reactions. These are the black swan signals.

### Framework B: Ury — Principled Negotiation (Getting to Yes)

William Ury's Harvard Negotiation Project framework. The counterpoint to Voss's
tactical empathy — strategic structure.

#### 1. Separate people from the problem
Don't negotiate positions; negotiate the problem. The other side is not your
enemy — the problem is.

#### 2. Focus on interests, not positions
Position: "We need a 20% discount." Interest: "We need to hit a budget target
this quarter." The interest reveals the real constraint — maybe you can offer
payment deferral instead of discount, solving their interest without giving
up price.

#### 3. Invent options for mutual gain
Don't assume the pie is fixed. Brainstorm options that expand value for both
sides. "What if we extended the contract term in exchange for the price you want?"

#### 4. Insist on objective criteria
Don't negotiate on willpower ("because I want it"). Negotiate on objective
criteria (market rates, comparable deals, cost-plus). "Industry standard for
this contract type is X. Can you show me data supporting your number?"

#### 5. Know your BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement)
Your BATNA is your walk-away power. If you don't know your BATNA, you can't
negotiate — you can only beg. Before any negotiation: "If this deal doesn't
happen, what's my best alternative?"

**BATNA rules**:
- Never accept a deal worse than your BATNA
- Improve your BATNA before negotiating (get competing offers)
- Don't reveal your BATNA — it sets your floor

### Framework C: Camp — Start with No

Jim Camp's contrarian framework. The opposite of "always be closing."

#### Core principles:
1. **Start with no**: Give the other side permission to say no. Reduces
   pressure, surfaces real objections.
2. **No neediness**: Neediness destroys negotiation power. You must be willing
   to walk away.
3. **No assumptions**: Don't assume what they want. Ask.
4. **Drive to "no"**: At every stage, ask "Is this a bad time?" or "Should we
   stop here?" This surfaces objections early.
5. **Decision cycles**: People decide in their own time. Don't force — nurture.

### Framework D: Cialdini — Influence (6 Principles of Persuasion)

Robert Cialdini's research on what makes people say yes. The psychology
underneath all negotiation.

#### 1. Reciprocity
People feel obligated to return favors. Give first — value, information,
concessions. The concession must be unsolicited and meaningful.

**Application**: In negotiation, make the first concession. They'll feel
obligated to reciprocate.

#### 2. Commitment & Consistency
People want to act consistently with their prior commitments. Get small
commitments early; they cascade into larger ones.

**Application**: Get the prospect to agree to small things ("you agree
onboarding takes 2 weeks?") before the big close. They'll stay consistent.

#### 3. Social Proof
People look to what others do when uncertain. Show that similar companies
use your product.

**Application**: "Companies like [their competitor] and [peer company] use
us for exactly this." Don't lie — but use real social proof.

#### 4. Authority
People defer to authority. Establish credibility early.

**Application**: Cite relevant data, name-drop advisors, show expertise.
Don't be self-aggrandizing — demonstrate, don't claim.

#### 5. Liking
People say yes to people they like. Build rapport before pitching.

**Application**: Find genuine common ground. Don't fake it — people detect
insincerity.

#### 6. Scarcity
People want what's scarce. Real scarcity (limited spots, time-bound offer)
drives action. Fake scarcity destroys trust.

**Application**: "We're onboarding 5 new customers this quarter — would you
like one of those slots?" (If true.)
</negotiation_frameworks>

## Part 2: Sales Methodologies

<sales_methodologies>

### Methodology A: SPIN Selling (Neil Rackham)

The most researched sales methodology — based on 35,000 calls analyzed by Huthwaite.

**SPIN = 4 question types**:

#### Situation questions
Establish the buyer's current state. Don't ask too many — buyers find them boring.

**Example**: "What tool are you currently using for X?"

#### Problem questions
Surface the buyer's pain. Make them articulate the problem before you propose a solution.

**Example**: "Are you satisfied with how [current tool] handles Y?" or "What's
the biggest challenge with your current process?"

#### Implication questions
Make the problem feel bigger. Connect the problem to its consequences.

**Example**: "If that process takes 2x longer than it should, how does that
affect your team's ability to hit quarterly targets?"

#### Need-payoff questions
Get the buyer to articulate the value of solving the problem. The buyer
should be selling themselves.

**Example**: "If we could reduce that process time by 50%, what would that
mean for your team?"

**Why SPIN works**: The buyer convinces themselves. You're not pitching —
you're facilitating their own realization.

### Methodology B: The Challenger Sale

Based on CEB research (now Gartner) — 6,000 sales reps across 90+ companies.
Counterintuitive finding: relationship builders are the WORST performers.
Challengers are the BEST.

**The 5 rep profiles**:
- Relationship builder (worst performer)
- Hard worker
- Lone wolf
- Reactive problem solver
- Challenger (best performer)

**Challenger = Teach, Tailor, Take Control**:

#### Teach
Teach the customer something new about their business. Bring a perspective
they don't have. Don't pitch your product — pitch a new insight about their
problem.

**Example**: "Most CFOs we work with didn't realize that [regulatory change]
creates a hidden $X cost in their compliance process. Here's the data."

#### Tailor
Customize the message to the specific stakeholder's priorities. The CFO
cares about cost; the COO cares about efficiency; the CISO cares about risk.
Same product, different message.

#### Take Control
Don't be passive. Push back on bad objections. Drive the process. Don't
let the customer control the pace — you control it.

### Methodology C: MEDDIC / MEDDPICC (Qualification Framework)

The enterprise sales qualification framework. Use to avoid wasting time on
deals that won't close.

**MEDDPICC = 8 qualifiers**:

1. **Metrics**: What number will this move for the customer? (e.g., "reduce
   compliance costs by $500K/yr")
2. **Economic buyer**: Who controls the budget? (Not always the user — find
   the person who can say yes without asking permission)
3. **Decision criteria**: What are their formal evaluation criteria? (Get
   this in writing)
4. **Decision process**: What's the procurement process? Who signs? How long?
5. **Paper process**: Legal, security review, MSAs — what are the steps
   after verbal yes?
6. **Identify pain**: What's the cost of not solving this? (Must be quantified)
7. **Champion**: Who inside the customer will fight for this deal? (Critical —
   no champion = no deal)
8. **Competition**: Who else are they evaluating? (Includes "do nothing")

**Rule**: If you can't answer all 8, the deal isn't qualified. Don't forecast it.

### Methodology D: Solution Selling vs Consultative Selling

#### Solution selling
Diagnose before prescribing. Ask diagnostic questions, then propose a
customized solution. Works for complex B2B.

#### Consultative selling
Become a trusted advisor. Don't push product — solve problems. Long sales
cycles, high ACV, relationship-driven.

**When to use which**:
- Transactional SaaS (<$10K ACV): Solution selling
- Mid-market ($10K-$100K ACV): Consultative
- Enterprise (>$100K ACV): Consultative + Challenger
</sales_methodologies>

## Part 3: Cold Outreach Playbook

<cold_outreach>

### Cold Email

#### Reply rate benchmarks
- Industry average: 1-3% (most cold email is bad)
- Good: 3-5%
- Excellent: 5-10%
- World-class: 10-20% (rare, requires extreme personalization)

#### The anatomy of a cold email that works

**Subject line** (3-5 words, no caps, no hype):
- "question about [specific thing they did]"
- "[their company] + [specific observation]"
- Avoid: "Quick question", "Following up", anything salesy

**Opening line** (prove you did your homework):
- "Saw your post about [specific topic] — the part about [specific detail]
  resonated because [specific reason]."
- NOT: "I hope this email finds you well" or "My name is X and I work at Y"

**Body** (3-4 sentences max):
- State the problem you solve (their problem, not your feature)
- Show 1 specific proof point (case study, metric, name-drop)
- Don't pitch features. Pitch outcomes.

**Call to action** (low-friction):
- "Worth a 15-min call next week?" (binary, easy yes)
- NOT: "I'd love to schedule a demo to show you our platform"

**Signature**:
- Name, title, company, link
- No inspirational quote, no logo image (kills deliverability)

#### Follow-up sequence
- Day 0: Initial email
- Day 3: Bump ("just floating this up")
- Day 7: Value-add (share a relevant resource, no pitch)
- Day 12: Breakup ("Assuming this isn't a priority — closing the loop")

**The "breakup email"** has the highest reply rate of the sequence (~20%).
Permission to say no = liberation to say yes.

### LinkedIn Outreach

#### Connection request
- Personalized note (max 300 chars)
- Reference specific shared context (group, post, mutual connection)
- Don't pitch in the connection request

#### After connection accepted
- Wait 2-3 days
- Send value first (resource, insight, introduction)
- Then ask for the call

### Cold Call

#### When cold call works
- B2B, high ACV (>$50K/yr)
- Defined ICP
- Phone numbers available (ZoomInfo, Apollo)

#### The 30-second opener
- "Hi [name], this is [your name] with [company]. I know I caught you cold —
  do you have 30 seconds for me to tell you why I called, and you can tell
  me to go away?"
- Permission-based opener triples talk time.

#### The pattern interrupt
- "Most [role] I talk to are struggling with [specific problem]. Is that on
  your radar?"
- Don't pitch. Elicit.
</cold_outreach>

## Part 4: Closing Techniques

<closing_techniques>

### 1. Trial close
Test readiness without asking for the close.
- "Does this look like it would solve your problem?"
- "If we can work out the pricing, are you ready to move forward?"

### 2. Assumptive close
Assume the close and discuss logistics.
- "Should we start the onboarding next week or the week after?"
- "What's the best email for the contract?"

### 3. Summary close
Summarize all the value they'll get, then ask.
- "So we're reducing your compliance cost by $X, saving Y hours/week, and
  eliminating the Z risk. Worth starting now?"

### 4. Alternative close
Give 2 options, both of which close.
- "Would you prefer annual billing at 20% off, or monthly?"

### 5. Urgency close (use carefully)
- "We have 2 onboarding slots left this month — should I hold one?"
- Only use real urgency. Fake urgency destroys trust.

### 6. Takeaway close
Reverse psychology — suggest maybe they shouldn't buy.
- "Honestly, based on what you've said about [constraint], maybe this isn't
  the right fit. We can revisit in 6 months."
- Often triggers "no wait, let's figure this out."

### 7. "No" close (Camp)
Push for "no" to surface the real objection.
- "Should we just close this out as not a fit?"
- "Is this a bad time to keep pursuing?"

### When NOT to close
- Bad fit customer (you'll regret the deal)
- Procurement hasn't been engaged (deal will die in legal)
- No champion identified (deal has no internal advocate)
- Economic buyer not involved (no budget authority)
- You're desperate (neediness kills deals)
</closing_techniques>

## Part 5: Buying Psychology

<buying_psychology>

### The Decision-Making Unit (DMU)

In B2B, no single person decides. The DMU has 5 roles:

1. **Champion**: Internal advocate who wants your product and fights for it.
   Without a champion, no deal. Identify and cultivate the champion first.
2. **Economic buyer**: Controls the budget. Can say yes without asking.
   Often not the user. Must be identified and sold separately.
3. **Technical buyer**: Evaluates whether your product meets technical
   requirements. Can veto, can't approve. Often IT/security.
4. **User**: Will actually use the product. Can influence, rarely decides.
5. **Coach**: Provides insider information about the deal. Not always a
   champion — sometimes just helpful.

**Multi-threaded selling**: Get meetings with 3+ DMU roles. Single-threaded
deals (only 1 contact) die when that contact leaves or loses interest.

### The "No Decision" Competitor

The biggest competitor in B2B isn't another vendor — it's "do nothing."
60-70% of B2B deals end in no decision, not loss to competitor.

**Why "no decision" wins**:
- Status quo bias (doing nothing feels safe)
- Lack of urgency (no deadline forcing action)
- Multiple stakeholders can't agree
- Cost of change feels higher than cost of problem
- Procurement friction

**How to beat "no decision"**:
- Quantify the cost of inaction (Lens 09 value-based pricing)
- Create urgency (regulatory deadline, budget cycle, competitor move)
- Reduce switching cost (free migration, white-glove onboarding)
- Identify and empower the champion to sell internally

### Buying biases to use ethically

#### Loss aversion
People fear loss more than they value equivalent gain. Frame as:
- "You're losing $X/month by not solving this" (more effective than
  "You'll save $X/month")

#### Status quo bias
Default to current state. Overcome by:
- Making the cost of status quo explicit and quantified
- Reducing perceived switching cost
- Showing peer companies that switched successfully

#### Anchoring
First number mentioned anchors the negotiation. Anchor high (you) before
they anchor low (them).

#### Social proof
"Companies like yours that switched from [incumbent] saw [specific outcome]."

#### Authority
Cite data, name-drop experts, demonstrate expertise. Don't claim authority —
earn it.
</buying_psychology>

## Part 6: Practitioner Wisdom

<practitioner_principles>

### 1. "He who cares least wins" — Camp
Neediness is the deal-killer. The side that can most easily walk away has
the most power. Build alternatives (BATNA) so you don't need any single deal.

### 2. "Seek first to understand, then to be understood" — Covey (via Voss)
Tactical empathy isn't soft — it's intelligence gathering. You can't
influence someone you don't understand. Mirror, label, ask calibrated
questions. Let them talk 70% of the time.

### 3. "Price is what you pay. Value is what you get." — Buffett
In negotiation, never let them frame it as price. Reframe as value.
"Don't focus on the $50K — focus on the $500K of cost it eliminates."

### 4. "The best deal is the one both sides feel slightly unhappy with." — Ury
If you got everything you wanted, you left money on the table. If they got
everything, you got squeezed. Slight mutual dissatisfaction = fair deal.

### 5. "No is the start of the negotiation, not the end." — Voss
"No" reveals constraints. A "no" tells you what to solve. Push for "no"
early to surface real objections. "Yes" is often counterfeit.

### 6. "You don't get what you deserve. You get what you negotiate." — Karrass
Hard truth. Good product doesn't win deals. Good negotiation does. Train
the skill.

### 7. "Discounting is a tax on poor salesmanship." — McKenzie (paraphrased)
Every discount teaches the customer your price is negotiable. Discount once,
they expect it forever. Hold price; offer value-add instead.

### 8. "The champion is the deal." — Enterprise sales wisdom
No champion, no deal. The champion sells internally when you're not in the
room. Identify the champion first, build the relationship, arm them with
 collateral to fight for you.

### 9. "Sell the problem, not the solution." — SPIN/Challenger
Don't pitch your product. Make the customer articulate their problem and
its cost. Then position your product as the solution to the problem they
just sold themselves on.

### 10. "Procurement is not your enemy — they're a gatekeeper with a job." — Enterprise sales wisdom
Procurement's job is to reduce spend and manage risk. Don't fight them —
help them. Give them the data they need to justify the deal internally.
Treat them as a stakeholder, not an obstacle.
</practitioner_principles>

## Few-Shot Examples

<examples>

### Example 1: Enterprise SaaS deal (positive)

<example>
<situation>
Founder selling $80K/yr SaaS to enterprise. Champion loves it, but economic buyer (CFO) is pushing back on price. "We can get [competitor] for $50K."
</situation>

<thinking>
The CFO is anchoring on competitor price ($50K). I need to reframe from price to value (Buffett principle). I also need to identify the CFO's real interest — probably budget pressure, not actual preference for competitor.

Voss technique: label the constraint. "It sounds like budget pressure this quarter is the real issue, not the total cost."

Ury technique: focus on interests, not positions. Position: "We need $30K off." Interest: "We need to hit a budget target this quarter."

Solution: offer payment deferral (Q2 start) at full price, or extended contract term (24 months) at slight discount. Solves the interest without giving up price.

If they insist on price cut: Camp's "no." "I understand if the budget doesn't work. We can revisit in Q2 when your fiscal year resets." Walk-away power. Often triggers them to find budget.

Don't discount. Discounting teaches them your price is negotiable (principle #7).
</thinking>

<answer>
Don't compete on price. Reframe as value and solve the underlying interest.

**Option 1 (best)**: Payment deferral. "I understand the Q1 budget constraint. What if we sign now at full price but start billing in Q2 when your new fiscal year opens? You lock in the rate, we both get the deal done."

**Option 2**: Extended term. "If 24-month commitment works for you, I can offer 10% off — that gets you close to your number without us discounting the annual rate."

**Option 3 (if they push for 30%+ off)**: Walk away. "I appreciate the conversation, but at that price point we can't deliver the quality of service you need. Let's revisit in Q2." Often triggers them to find budget they "didn't have."

**Critical**: Don't discount once and walk away. That teaches them your price is negotiable and they'll expect it every renewal.
</answer>
</example>

### Example 2: Cold email rewrite (positive)

<example>
<situation>
Founder's cold email getting 1% reply rate. Current email: "Hi [name], I'm [founder] at [company]. We make [product] that helps [generic benefit]. Would love to show you a demo. Are you free next week?"
</situation>

<thinking>
This email violates every principle:
- Subject line probably generic ("Quick question")
- Opens with "I'm X at Y" (about the sender, not the recipient)
- Generic benefit (no specific pain identified)
- Asks for demo (high friction)
- "Are you free next week" (open-ended, easy to ignore)

Rewrite using Voss (tactical empathy), SPIN (problem first), Cialdini (personalization = liking):

Subject: "your post about [specific topic]"
Opening: reference something specific they did
Body: name a specific problem they likely have (based on research)
CTA: low-friction 15-min call

Personalization is the unlock. The founder is sending 100 emails/day with no research. Better to send 10/day with 5 minutes of research each.
</thinking>

<answer>
Rewrite:

**Subject**: your post about [specific topic they wrote about]

**Body**:
"[Name] — saw your post about [specific topic]. The part about [specific detail] resonated because [specific reason showing you actually read it].

Most [their role] I talk to are hitting a wall with [specific problem related to what they wrote about]. The cost is usually [specific quantified impact — time, money, risk].

We helped [peer company] solve exactly this — [specific outcome, e.g., "cut their compliance review time by 60%"]. Worth a 15-min call next week?

[Name]"

**Why this works**:
- Subject is specific (not "quick question")
- Opening proves you did homework (liking principle)
- Body names a problem (SPIN — problem before solution)
- Proof point is specific (social proof)
- CTA is low-friction (15 min, not demo)
- No mention of your product features (sell the problem)

**Send volume**: 10 highly personalized emails/day beats 100 generic ones. 5-10% reply rate vs 1%.
</answer>
</example>
</examples>

## Decision Protocol

<decision_protocol>
### Exact Question
"How do I sell this and negotiate terms that capture value?"

### Data Required
- ICP definition (who buys, why, how)
- Value quantification (Lens 09)
- Competitor pricing (Lens 10)
- Buyer DMU mapped (champion, economic buyer, technical, user, coach)
- BATNA identified (your walk-away)

### Confidence Threshold
- **Deploy (run the sales process)**: ≥75% confidence, ICP defined, value quantified, DMU mapped
- **Flag (proceed with caveats)**: 50-75%, ICP fuzzy, value not quantified
- **Discard**: <50%, no clear buyer, no value quantification

### Conflict Resolution
- Lens 14 (Sales) + Lens 09 (Pricing): Pricing sets the number; sales negotiates around it. Don't let sales discount below pricing floor.
- Lens 14 + Lens 13 (Growth): Sales process is the growth motion for SLG. PLG doesn't need Lens 14 as heavily.
- Lens 14 + Lens 10 (Competitor): Competitor intel informs positioning. Don't badmouth competitors — reframe.
</decision_protocol>
