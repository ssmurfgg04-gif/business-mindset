# Mental Models Framework — A Latticework for Business Thinking

Distilled from Charlie Munger's "latticework of mental models," Daniel
Kahneman's cognitive psychology, Richard Feynman's first-principles thinking,
and systems thinking (Senge, Meadows). This framework provides the
foundational thinking tools that the skill uses across all lenses.

## Core Philosophy

<principle>
"You must know the big ideas in the big disciplines and use them routinely
— all of them, not just a few. Most people are trained in one model and
try to solve all problems with that one model." — Charlie Munger

The skill doesn't use one mental model. It uses a latticework — multiple
models from multiple disciplines, applied contextually. This is what
distinguishes top 1% operators from average ones: they have more models
and know which to apply when.
</principle>

## The 5 Disciplines of Mental Models

<disciplines>

### Discipline 1: Decision-Making Frameworks

#### 1. OODA Loop (John Boyd)
**Observe → Orient → Decide → Act**

Developed for fighter pilots, applied to business. The operator who cycles
through OODA fastest wins. Speed of decision-making is a competitive advantage.

- **Observe**: gather information without judgment
- **Orient**: filter through your mental models, culture, experience
- **Decide**: make a reversible decision (70% information is enough)
- **Act**: execute, then observe the result (restart loop)

**Application**: In fast-moving markets, the operator who OODAs faster
wins. Bezos's "Day 1" mentality = fast OODA loops.

#### 2. Pre-mortem (Gary Klein)
**Before acting, assume failure.**

"Assume it's 6 months from now. This decision failed. What caused it?"

Forces you to surface risks that optimism hides. Used in Lens 06 pre-mortem
mandatory step.

#### 3. Expected Value (EV)
**EV = (probability of outcome) × (payoff if outcome)**

Every decision should be evaluated on EV, not best-case or worst-case. Most
people think in best-case (optimism bias) or worst-case (loss aversion). EV
thinking forces you to weight by probability.

**Application**: Lens 11 opportunity sifting uses EV to rank opportunities.

#### 4. Kelly Criterion
**Optimal bet size = (probability × payoff ratio) - (1 - probability) / payoff ratio**

Tells you what fraction of your bankroll to bet. Never bet full Kelly (too
risky). Half-Kelly or quarter-Kelly is standard. Used in Lens 08 (Risk of Ruin).

#### 5. Bezos's Type 1 vs Type 2 Decisions
- **Type 1 (irreversible)**: Decide slowly, ~90% information. Marriage, M&A, big bets.
- **Type 2 (reversible)**: Decide fast, ~70% information. Most decisions are Type 2.

Most founders treat Type 2 decisions as Type 1 — they're too slow.

#### 6. Inversion (Munger/Jacobi)
**Don't ask "how to succeed." Ask "how to fail." Avoid that.**

"All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I never go there." — Munger

For every opportunity, run a pre-mortem. What kills it? Avoid those things.

#### 7. Bayesian Updating
**Update your beliefs as new evidence arrives.**

Prior probability + new evidence = posterior probability. Don't hold fixed
beliefs — update them as you learn. Most people fail at this (confirmation
bias).

#### 8. Circle of Competence (Buffett/Munger)
**Know what you know and what you don't.**

Stay in your circle. But relentlessly expand the circle by learning. The
biggest mistakes come from operating outside your circle of competence
without realizing it.

### Discipline 2: Systems Thinking

#### 9. Feedback Loops
- **Positive (reinforcing) feedback**: more users → better product → more
  users (network effects, Lens 07 Signal 6)
- **Negative (balancing) feedback**: more users → slower service → fewer
  users (capacity constraints)

Every business is a system of feedback loops. Map them. Reinforcing loops
are growth engines; balancing loops are growth limits.

#### 10. Leverage Points (Donella Meadows)
**Places to intervene in a system, ordered by effectiveness:**

1. Paradigm (most leverage)
2. Goals
3. Rules
4. Information flow
5. Material flow
6. Numbers (least leverage)

Most operators work on numbers (least leverage). The best work on paradigms
and rules (most leverage).

#### 11. Unintended Consequences
**Every intervention has second-order effects.**

"The government solution to a problem is usually as bad as the problem." — Milton Friedman

In business: every feature shipped, every hire made, every channel added
has unintended consequences. Surface them with pre-mortems.

#### 12. Bottlenecks (Theory of Constraints)
**Every system has one bottleneck. Improving anything else is wasted effort.**

Identify the constraint. Improve the constraint. Repeat. (Goldratt's "The Goal")

**Application**: If sales is the bottleneck, more engineers won't help. If
onboarding is the bottleneck, more leads won't help. Find the constraint.

#### 13. Compounding
**Small advantages multiply over time.**

"The first $100K is the hardest." — Buffett

Compounding applies to:
- Capital (interest on interest)
- Audience (each subscriber brings more)
- Brand (each customer strengthens reputation)
- Knowledge (each insight connects to others)

Compounding is the most powerful force in business. But it requires time
horizons (Lens 13 Stage 1+: don't sacrifice compounding for short-term growth).

### Discipline 3: Cognitive Biases (Kahneman)

#### 14. System 1 vs System 2 (Kahneman)
- **System 1**: fast, intuitive, emotional, automatic (gut reactions)
- **System 2**: slow, deliberate, logical, effortful (analysis)

Most decisions are System 1. Most IMPORTANT decisions should be System 2.
Force System 2 engagement by writing down reasoning (Bezos narrative memo).

#### 15. Loss Aversion
**People fear loss 2x more than they value equivalent gain.**

In sales: "You're losing $X/month" beats "You'll save $X/month."
In pricing: customers fight price increases more than they value price decreases.
In hiring: people stay in bad jobs longer than they should (loss aversion to leaving).

#### 16. Anchoring
**The first number mentioned anchors all subsequent thinking.**

In negotiation: anchor first (you) or refuse their anchor and reframe.
In pricing: the first price they see anchors willingness to pay. Show
expensive tier first (decoy effect).

#### 17. Confirmation Bias
**We seek evidence that confirms our beliefs and ignore disconfirming evidence.**

Most dangerous bias in business. Counter with:
- Disconfirming evidence search (Lens 06 mandatory)
- Adversarial audit (red team)
- Pre-mortem (assume you're wrong)

#### 18. Sunk Cost Fallacy
**We continue failing projects because of past investment.**

Past investment is irrelevant to future expected value. Ask: "Would I start
this today if I hadn't already invested?"

#### 19. Survivorship Bias
**We study successes and ignore failures.**

Most "how I built X" content is survivorship-biased. The 100 people who did
the same thing and failed didn't write blog posts. Counter by searching for
failures (Lens 06 disconfirming evidence search).

#### 20. Availability Heuristic
**We judge probability by how easily examples come to mind.**

Recent, vivid, emotional events feel more probable than they are. Counter
with base rate thinking: "Of 100 opportunities like this, how many succeed?"

#### 21. Dunning-Kruger Effect
**Incompetent people overestimate their competence. Competent people underestimate.**

In business: founders often overestimate their skill in domains they're new
to. Counter with calibration tracking (Lens 06 decision journal).

#### 22. Planning Fallacy
**We underestimate time and cost, overestimate benefits.**

Most projects take 2x as long and cost 2x as much as planned. Add 50% buffer
to every estimate. Pre-mortem what could delay.

#### 23. Halo Effect
**One positive trait colors perception of everything else.**

A well-designed website makes people assume the product is good. A famous
founder makes investors assume the company will succeed. Counter by
separating traits: evaluate each independently.

### Discipline 4: Strategy Frameworks

#### 24. Porter's Five Forces
**Industry attractiveness determined by 5 forces:**
1. Threat of new entrants
2. Bargaining power of suppliers
3. Bargaining power of buyers
4. Threat of substitutes
5. Competitive rivalry

Attractive industries: low threats, low rivalry. Use to evaluate market
entry (Lens 10 competitor teardown).

#### 25. Porter's Generic Strategies
**3 ways to compete:**
1. **Cost leadership** (Walmart, Amazon) — lowest cost
2. **Differentiation** (Apple, Nike) — unique value
3. **Focus** (niche) — narrow segment, either cost or differentiation

Stuck in the middle (no clear strategy) = death. Pick one.

#### 26. Christensen's Disruption Theory
**Incumbents lose to disruptors when:**
- Disruptor enters low-end or new market
- Incumbent can't respond without cannibalizing existing business
- Disruptor improves until it serves the mainstream

Used in Lens 10 (Competitor Teardown) counter-positioning analysis.

#### 27. Helmer's 7 Powers
**7 sources of durable competitive advantage:**
1. Scale
2. Network
3. Counter-positioning
4. Switching costs
5. Branding
6. Cornered resource
7. Process power

Used in Lens 10 to score competitor defensibility.

#### 28. Jobs to Be Done (Christensen)
**Customers don't buy products. They hire them to do jobs.**

Find the actual job (not the official job). The gap between official and
actual job is the vulnerability (Lens 10).

#### 29. Aggregation Theory (Ben Thompson)
**Aggregators control demand; linear businesses control supply.**

Aggregators (Google, Facebook, Uber) win by commoditizing suppliers. Linear
businesses (old media, traditional SaaS) win by controlling supply. Used in
Lens 10 to determine attack vector.

### Discipline 5: Mental Models from Other Disciplines

#### 30. Critical Mass (Physics)
**The point where a reaction becomes self-sustaining.**

In business: the point where network effects kick in and growth compounds.
For marketplaces: liquidity (enough buyers + sellers). For social: network
density (enough connections).

#### 31. Inertia (Physics)
**Objects in motion stay in motion; objects at rest stay at rest.**

Incumbents have inertia — hard to pivot. Startups have low inertia — can
pivot fast. Use as advantage.

#### 32. Evolution (Biology)
**Adaptation to environment determines survival.**

Markets evolve. Businesses that don't adapt die. "It is not the strongest
of the species that survives, nor the most intelligent, but the one most
responsive to change." (Attributed to Darwin, possibly misattributed, but
principle holds.)

#### 33. Ecological Niche (Biology)
**Species succeed by finding an underexploited niche.**

In business: find a segment incumbents under-serve (Christensen disruption).
Don't compete head-on; find your niche.

#### 34. Opportunity Cost (Economics)
**The cost of one choice is the next-best alternative.**

Every hour spent on X is an hour not spent on Y. Ask: "Is this the highest-
value use of my time?" If not, what is?

#### 35. Comparative Advantage (Economics)
**Even if you're worse at everything, you should specialize in your
comparative advantage.**

If you're 80% as good at design and 50% as good at engineering, specialize
in design (your comparative advantage) and partner with an engineer. Don't
try to do both.

#### 36. Game Theory (Economics)
**Strategic decisions must account for other players' responses.**

In competitive markets: model what competitors will do in response to your
move. Don't assume they'll stay still (Lens 10 incumbent reaction analysis).

#### 37. Margin of Safety (Engineering)
**Design for 2-3x the expected load.**

Bridges are designed for 3x expected traffic. Businesses should have 2-3x
the runway they think they need. Things go wrong; margin of safety prevents
catastrophe.

#### 38. Redundancy (Engineering)
**Critical systems have backup.**

Single points of failure kill. In business: don't have one customer, one
channel, one supplier, one key employee. Build redundancy.

#### 39. Regression to the Mean (Statistics)
**Extreme outcomes tend to be followed by less extreme outcomes.**

Outstanding quarters are followed by average quarters. Terrible quarters
are followed by less terrible ones. Don't over-interpret single data points.

#### 40. Power Law (Statistics)
**In many domains, 80% of outcomes come from 20% of causes.**

- 80% of revenue from 20% of customers
- 80% of impact from 20% of features
- 80% of returns from 20% of investments (VC)

Focus on the 20% that matters. Don't optimize the long tail.
</disciplines>

## How to Apply Mental Models

<application>

### The Latticework Approach

Don't use one model. Use multiple. For any decision:

1. **What models apply?** (List 3-5)
2. **What do they each say?** (Different models may disagree)
3. **Where do they converge?** (Strong signal)
4. **Where do they diverge?** (Investigate further)

**Example**: Evaluating whether to raise VC.
- **EV thinking**: VC raises expected outcome if Tier 1, lowers if Tier 2-3
- **Circle of competence**: Do you know how to manage VC relationships?
- **Margin of safety**: Does VC give 18+ months runway?
- **Game theory**: How will competitors respond to your accelerated growth?
- **Loss aversion**: Are you raising VC because you fear running out of cash
  (loss aversion) rather than because it's EV-positive?

Multiple models converge → strong decision. Models diverge → investigate.

### Common Combinations

| Decision | Models to combine |
|---|---|
| Opportunity evaluation | EV + Kelly + Inversion + Survivorship |
| Pricing | Anchoring + Loss aversion + Comparative advantage |
| Competitive response | Game theory + Inertia + Disruption theory |
| Hiring | Halo effect + Dunning-Kruger + Margin of safety |
| Capital allocation | EV + Opportunity cost + Compounding + Regression to mean |
| Growth strategy | Feedback loops + Critical mass + Bottlenecks + Power law |

### The Anti-Pattern: Single-Model Thinking

Most people are trained in one model (their profession) and apply it to
everything. Engineers think everything is an engineering problem. Marketers
think everything is a marketing problem. Finance people think everything is
a finance problem.

The skill avoids this by having access to all 40 models and applying them
contextually. This is the latticework.

</application>
